on the dispute of science vs art

April 21, 2026 · lingerings

On the supposed dispute between science and art, I feel uniquely qualified to call the entire thing what it is: stupid.

As a pre-med whose life is rooted in science, but who has also been recognized for creative writing, I have lived too closely in both worlds to buy into the performance either side insists on staging. Science people like to imply they are above the arts—more rigorous, more objective, more intelligent. Arts people, in turn, claim a different kind of superiority: that they are more soulful, more human, more alive.

Science gives art precision, structure, and explanatory depth. Art gives science meaning, accessibility, and human weight. They do not merely coexist; they refine each other. At any meaningful level, each depends on the other to fully develop. So when people stage this tired war between science and art, it doesn’t read as profound disagreement. It’s stupid like watching two hands argue over which one matters more to the body.

Let me establish my ethos before anyone with a superiority complex starts talking. I do Parkinson’s research. I look at brain scans for fun. I spend my time thinking about neural connections, signaling pathways, and the relationship between muscle fibers and the central nervous system. I am not speaking as someone who is vaguely “into science.” I am speaking as someone whose daily life is buried in one of the most technical, fetishized, and allegedly intellectual fields there is. And the deeper you go into the brain, the less plausible it becomes that science and art are separate at all.

To study neuroscience is to confront, over and over, the fact that a three-pound organ suspended in fluid and darkness can produce grief, language, music, hallucination, prayer, desire, memory, morality—and the entire fiction of the self. Ion gradients and membrane potentials become a mother recognizing her child’s cry instantly, or a poet pulled back into childhood by the smell of rain. Understanding the mechanism does not diminish the experience; it deepens it.

So when people posture as if they’ve transcended art, they don’t sound rigorous. They sound socially underdeveloped. Neuroscience, one of the most prestigious corners of medicine, relies on perception, metaphor, interpretation, and meaning at every level. Pretending otherwise is not objectivity. It is arrogance with bad writing.

The Mona Lisa

The Mona Lisa is not just the most famous painting in the world. It is one of the clearest, and most inconvenient, rebuttals to the idea that art and science exist in separate universes. Leonardo da Vinci did not paint her despite science. He painted her with it. It forces a simple question: what, exactly, is “art” here that is not already entangled with perception, anatomy, and optics?

Her gaze is the first clue. People describe it as mystical, as if she has some supernatural ability to follow you across the room. But that “mystery” is not magic. It is optics, geometry, and human perception working so seamlessly together that technical mastery gets mistaken for the supernatural. Da Vinci positioned her eyes and structured the composition with such precision that your brain completes the effect. Human beings are wired to respond to forward-facing eyes with immediate psychological intensity. We are animals before we are intellectuals. We feel watched because our biology tells us to.

Then there is the smile. Look directly at her mouth, and it seems to flatten. Look away, and it returns, faint, ambiguous, almost taunting. That is not mystery. That is visual processing. He understood, centuries before formal cognitive science, that the eye does not read an image uniformly. The fovea captures detail; peripheral vision absorbs softness and shadow. Through sfumato, those thin, smoky layers of paint, he manipulated that imbalance with precision, embedding ambiguity into the mechanics of sight itself. One of the most iconic emotional effects in art history depends on how human vision works. Art is not standing apart from science here. It is operating through it.

This was not instinctive genius falling from the sky. Da Vinci dissected cadavers. He studied anatomy obsessively—tracing facial muscles, mapping proportion, examining how expression is physically produced: how a lip lifts, how a cheek tightens, how the muscles around the mouth and eyes distinguish a dead expression from a living one. He understood that emotion is not some vague spiritual mist floating above the body. It is built into tension, structure, movement, and flesh. The smile works because he knew what a face actually does when it almost smiles. He studied the body like a scientist so he could render feeling like an artist.

Even the landscape conspires in her strangeness. The horizon behind her is uneven—the left side sits lower than the right. Because the visual system uses the horizon as a cue for levelness and bodily orientation, that imbalance subtly alters how her shoulders and torso are perceived. The two sides of the background imply slightly different spatial levels, and the brain is forced to reconcile them. It cannot quite settle. Is she sitting straight? Is one shoulder lifted? Is her torso turning? Her body becomes harder to “lock” into a single, stable position. That continuous adjustment produces the sense that she is not fully still, but in the act of settling into stillness. The landscape is not just background. It creates motion out of stasis, making her feel less like an image and more like a body in space.

She feels alive. The reason is not mystery. It is science.

What feels like magic in art is often just mastery we don’t recognize. And that pattern doesn’t stop at painting. It extends into music, where the same mechanisms—expectation, perception, and disruption—operate in sound instead of image.

Music does the same thing, just in time instead of space.

People love to pretend their taste is a moral achievement.

Play Beethoven, and posture improves. Play Travis Scott, and suddenly people are diagnosing cultural decline. One is granted genius before the first movement ends; the other is dismissed before the bass has a chance to drop. In that reflex—reverence on one side, contempt on the other—you can see how badly many people misunderstand both music and themselves.

A musical note is an expressive event, but it is also a physical one. It is a vibration moving through the air, measurable in frequency, amplitude, and duration. Harmony is not just a poetic idea; it emerges from relationships among sound waves and from the way the auditory system processes tension and resolution. Rhythm is not simply something we “feel.” It depends on timing, repetition, pattern, and the brain’s ability to anticipate what comes next. None of this makes music less powerful. It explains where that power comes from. Music moves us not despite its mechanics, but through them.

No one leaves a performance of Ludwig van Beethoven talking about acoustics or neural prediction. They leave moved. But being moved is not proof that music floats above the material world. It is proof of how deeply the material world can act on us. The ear detects pattern, the brain predicts continuation, and the body responds when those predictions are fulfilled, delayed, or disrupted. What we call transcendence often arrives through entirely physical means: pressure waves, neural signals, muscular tension, a quickened pulse.

Take Symphony No. 3 (Eroica). It opens with two massive E-flat major chords, a public declaration of order, confidence, and authority. And then Beethoven unsettles it. He introduces instability into the very space he has just created, forcing the listener to adjust. That tension is part of what gives the piece its force. Beethoven does not merely satisfy the ear. He teaches the listener how to hear the piece, then disrupts that expectation, pulling the music into less stable, less comfortable territory.

Now consider SICKO MODE. It operates through many of the same principles. It establishes a groove—a pulse, a sonic environment—long enough for the listener to settle into it, and then it swerves. The beat switches are not incidental tricks; they are central to the track’s design. They force recalibration. They turn instability into expectation. They make surprise the engine of the experience. This is not the absence of composition. It is composition working through different tools.

And yet many listeners still struggle to hear that, because they mistake familiarity for depth. They have learned to recognize complexity only when it arrives in the approved costume of seriousness. Strings, pianos, European surnames, old concert halls—these still trigger an automatic assumption of cultural value. Bass, repetition, swagger, 808s—these still trigger suspicion. That is not neutral discernment. It is how class prejudice reproduces itself as taste, and how racial hierarchy persists in the language of standards.

To say this is not to deny the differences between symphonic form and rap production, or to claim that all music is interchangeable. It is to insist on something simpler: formal intelligence does not belong to one tradition, one class, one race, or one set of instruments. Complexity can emerge through harmonic development, but also through rhythm, texture, layering, bass pressure, interruption, and rupture. The body does not stop being a body because the music is played in a club rather than a concert hall.

Art is not what escapes science. It is what science feels like from the inside. And science is not what strips art of its meaning. It is what reveals how that meaning is made. So the question is no longer which one matters more. The question is why anyone still believes they can be separated at all.
