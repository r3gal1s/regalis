Here Lie the Tomorrows, Buried Beside the Sons

"The wars will end, the leaders will shake hands, the old woman will remain waiting for her martyred son, that girl will wait for her beloved husband, and the children will wait for their heroic father, I do not know who sold the homeland but I know who paid the price."
– Mahmoud Darwish, Palestinian poet.

Someday, the artillery fire will cease, and the battlefield will settle into a haunted stillness. The treaties will be drawn, peace accords signed, and leaders will stand before the flashing cameras, their polished hands clasped warmly, as if the blood beneath could easily wash away. There will be applause, and talk of healing, and grand speeches about unity and reconciliation. And for a brief, dazzling moment, the world will sigh in relief, believing perhaps the nightmare has ended at last.

In the border town of Mae Sot, life collects in the spaces between things, in alleys behind market stalls, under rusted zinc roofs that drip during the monsoon. Smoke curls from open cookfires beside concrete walls painted with peeling cartoon murals. Children chase deflated footballs through puddles. Radios buzz with static and voices from home that sound farther away each day.

An old woman sits at her worn doorstep, eyes hollowed with grief, heart heavy with waiting. She listens to the wind,  not for birdsong, but for boots on gravel, the click of a latch, the voice that once called her Mama. He was taken in the bloom of youth, handed a rifle, and told he was fighting for freedom. There was no body to bury. Just a medal in a box and a name carved into cold stone. Every knock at the door stops her heart. Every footstep stirs the ghost of a hope she dares not kill.

A young woman smooths the wrinkles from her husband’s faded shirt. She sets a table for two. His toothbrush stands in a plastic cup by the sink. She only sleeps on the right side of the bed. .

They press ripped and crumpled photographs to their cheeks as if warmth could be drawn from paper. Some cry. Others go quiet, learning too early how to swallow grief. .

In one cramped room behind a tire shop, a mother folds school uniforms her children have long since outgrown. The fabric still smells faintly of chalk dust and old ambition. Outside, her teenage son straddles a battered motorcycle, tapping the throttle in short bursts. His helmet lies forgotten beside the door. .

He joins a group of teenagers loitering outside an NGO-funded learning center. The walls are bare, the desks uneven, and during storms the mud pushes in through the cracked floor. A girl sits with a workbook open but blank, the pencil resting untouched between her fingers. She stares at the page like it might punish her for dreaming too long.

These children mourn not only what was lost, but what might never be possible again.

In a classroom in Yangon with chalk-streaked walls and buzzing fluorescent lights, she built bridges from paper straws and tape, her hands steady, her mind racing through load-bearing calculations. She switched between Burmese, English, and Japanese with effortless ease, conjugating verbs while sketching cantilevers. Now she calculates how many grams of rice are left for her family’s dinner. Her fingers grip the fraying handles of water jugs instead of models and rulers. 

In the fields near the refugee camp, men dig latrines by hand. A college professor, who once recited Chaucer and Orwell from memory and debated the nuances of postcolonial literature, now crouches silently in the muck, sliding PVC pipes into place. He doesn’t speak much these days. His hands blister easily. His daughter once stood at the front of the class, reciting Yeats with a voice that could command a room. Now she hardly speaks above a whisper. Even her laughter sounds like a memory.

The children who once memorized world capitals and debugged code in Python now memorize silence. They’ve learned how to count seconds between gunshots, to read the tremble in an adult’s voice, to measure danger in footsteps. They don’t need calendars to know the days. They mark time by the number of rice cups left, the number of faces missing at evening roll call. .

The rain starts again, tapping like fingers against tarps and tents. Motorcycles slash through the dark, headlights broken, engines rasping like breath. Tires spin on gravel and mud. The riders laugh as they pass, but it’s not joy you hear. It’s a razor-edged sound, the kind born from defiance pretending to be fun. They race not because they want to die, but because they’ve stopped believing that caution saves anyone. .

But how can we blame them?

They’ve learned the rules don’t protect the rule-followers. They’ve seen what happens to the quiet ones. They’ve seen soldiers drag children into trucks, seen families disappear between dusk and dawn. They’ve seen classrooms crumble into detention cells, blackboards shattered and books scattered like fallen leaves.

Every gold star, every memorized speech, every equation scribbled in the margins of a schoolbook now lies forgotten in a file that will never be opened. Their schools are barricaded. Their teachers have disappeared. Their classmates are spread across borders, or buried beneath collapsed walls. They’ve watched dreams smolder and curl into smoke.

They don’t say it out loud. But they avoid talking about next week. Next month. Next year. As if the very act of planning is dangerous. As if hope itself might betray them.  Because once you’ve watched people your age light the match that burns your home, once you’ve stuffed your entire childhood into a single plastic bag and crossed a river under the cold gaze of the moon. 

What is there left to believe in?

So no, we cannot blame them.

Not the boy who falls asleep during class, because he stayed awake the night before, listening for footsteps. Not the girl who won’t speak during discussion, because silence is the only safe language she knows. Not the ones who laugh too loudly at things that aren’t funny, or stare too long at the wall, lost in something they no longer name.

They are not careless. They are grieving. They are not hopeless.

They are guarding the last fragile thread of hope by pretending it no longer matters. Because if everything you love can be destroyed, without warning, without mercy,  Then maybe, just maybe, it hurts less not to love anything at all.
