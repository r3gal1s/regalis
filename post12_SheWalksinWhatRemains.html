She Walks in What Remains

The jungle was thick with breath and memory.

Every night, as the cicadas screamed and the air clung to her skin like guilt, she wondered if this would be the night they found her. Or the night the sorrow would finally catch up to her body, as it already had to her spirit.

She slept beneath a tattered tarp among bamboo groves at the border between two countries, one she had fled, one she would never truly enter. Close enough to her homeland to hear it weep. But never close enough to touch.

Once, she had stood in Parliament. Her voice, once strong and steady, had echoed through marble halls. She had been the youngest woman elected from her district, a beacon for democracy. Her mother called her “a flame too bright for a dark room.” But the flame had drawn shadows. And when the generals rose like storm clouds, swallowing ballots and silencing voices, they came for her first.

She fled with the clothes on her back and a name on a watchlist.

The clothes she wore now, faded, rain-beaten, and patched by her own trembling hands, were all that remained of that other life. She hadn’t bought a new piece of clothing in four years. Not since her sister died.

Her last blouse, the one with delicate sky-blue stitching along the sleeves, had been chosen by that sister. Everything was, really. She had never had an eye for fashion. Her world had been built from campaign posters and constitution drafts, gray suits and khaki slacks, practical, forgettable. It was her sister who softened her, wrapped her in sarongs with floral prints, and gently tugged her toward color.

“You can change the world and still wear something with a little joy,” her sister used to say, holding fabric up to her face with that teasing grin.

It was her sister who had chosen the golden shawl for her first major speech. Who pinned a brooch to her lapel and whispered, “Smile, sis—today you’re going to change the country.”

Now there were no brooches. No speeches. Only the jungle, the silence, and the weight of wearing something that once made her look brave.

Now it just made her feel haunted.

Six gone in a single year. One after the other, like stars winking out over a dark sea. First the eldest brother. A heart attack, they said. The weight of her absence crushed him as much as the years of carrying their family. Then his wife, two months later. Grief, or maybe the regime. They blurred together now.

Then her mother, who had waited every day by the door, a shrine in her eyes. “She asked if you’d come back for the festival,” her sister had whispered over a call so full of static it sounded like wind. “She wanted you to light the incense.”

She had lit incense, yes, but in the jungle, for no gods she trusted anymore. Only ghosts. Hers.

Then the other brother. His wife. And finally, her sister.

Fever took her. Fast. Alone.

The same sister who used to braid jasmine in her hair and say, “Let’s grow old protesting together.”

Each time someone died, she considered going back. Each time, she pictured what would happen if she did. Surveillance. Arrests. Retaliation against the few family members still clinging to safety. Grief had become a weapon. And the junta wielded it with precision. To mourn publicly was to endanger others. To return was to betray the living.

So she stayed. And she carried it.

What haunted her wasn’t only the dying—but the not being there. Not brushing her mother’s hair one last time. Not holding her brother’s hand in the hospital. Not singing lullabies to her sister as the fever burned through her. Not digging the graves with her own hands.

The jungle wrapped around her heart as tightly as it wrapped around her body—humid, choking, inescapable.

And her clothes—fraying at the hems, stiff from rain—were her stitched-together shrine. Every shirt, every skirt, still whispered her sister’s voice. Letting them go would mean choosing something new without her. It would mean moving forward, even just an inch. She wasn’t ready for that.

She wore them again and again. Mended them in the dark with fingers stiff from cold nights. No one noticed. In exile, survival was the uniform. No one asked why her clothes never changed.

Now, in the shadowed hush of another dawn, she sat by a stream, its water the color of ash. The jungle around her hadn’t yet stirred—not with birdsong, not with the chatter of insects. Just the quiet murmur of water, as if the earth itself was holding its breath.

Beside her, a bamboo bowl bobbed gently in the shallows, filled with crushed jasmine and sandalwood—fragile offerings to lives lost and promises broken. She cupped it with both hands for a moment before letting it go, watching it drift into the slow, silken current. The petals quivered like they too were uncertain whether to stay or surrender.

She leaned forward, elbows on her knees, and began to whisper.

One name. Then another. And another.

Each name tasted different on her tongue—some sharp and fresh with pain, others worn smooth by time. But all of them left a residue. She could feel it settling in her chest, thick and heavy, as though her lungs had learned to breathe grief instead of air.

“I’m sorry,” she said into the silence. Her voice barely rose above a murmur. “I couldn’t bury you. But you bury me every day.”

She had no nation now. No home. No headstones to kneel before. Just a patch of earth borrowed from the border and the ghosts that had followed her into exile. Ghosts she fed with jasmine and guilt.

A single tear broke free, cutting a path down her cheek. She watched it fall into the dirt, darkening it for a second before disappearing. Then, almost reflexively, her weathered hand rose and wiped it away.

There would be time to mourn after they had won the war.

This wasn’t the end. It couldn’t be.

The jungle was waking, and with it, the day’s weight. Another meeting with runners. Another encrypted message to record. Another camp to move before the drones circled back. She gathered herself slowly, as if rising from prayer.

She wrapped the fraying shawl tighter around her shoulders—the one her sister had picked out years ago, bright once, now dulled to the color of old gold. She didn’t look back at the water.

There would be time. Someday.

For now, she walked into the jungle.

Not to forget.

But to keep going.
